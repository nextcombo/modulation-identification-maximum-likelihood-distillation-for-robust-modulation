# [Modulation Identification] Maximum Likelihood Distillation for Robust Modulation Classification [with Python Code]

1. Abstract: Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) are widely used in communication systems, especially in the field of automatic modulation classification (AMC). However, such networks are highly susceptible to carefully designed small adversarial perturbations that can alter their decision-making outcomes. This study aims to build a more robust AMC system based on knowledge distillation and adversarial training.
Firstly, this paper elucidates the importance of training data quality for model accuracy and robustness; subsequently, it proposes generating better training labels using the maximum likelihood (ML) function, which can solve the AMC problem offline. These labels allow the model to exhibit uncertainty in complex environments, and combined with adversarial training, they can simultaneously improve both the accuracy and robustness of the model.
Interestingly, the study found that performance improvements can be transferred to online scenarios—where the maximum likelihood function cannot be directly applied. Overall, this research emphasizes that overcoming label noise directly is less effective than letting the model learn to exhibit uncertainty in complex scenarios.
2. Introduction: Communication systems typically require receivers to identify the modulation scheme used for encoding transmission signals, known as automatic modulation classification (AMC), which has wide applications, including everyday radio station detection, spectrum resource management, and wireless communication eavesdropping and interference. The AMC task can be transformed into a maximum likelihood (ML) estimation problem [1]-[3], where the likelihood function is maximized over all possible modulation schemes by calculating the received signal's likelihood under all possible modulation schemes. Although this solution adheres to the Bayesian optimal criterion, it often resorts to suboptimal approximations due to the need for prior knowledge of channel characteristics and the high computational complexity of real-time solution.
Deep learning is considered to be a superior solution for AMC: neural networks can quickly perform inference, adapt to any channel characteristics, and significantly improve performance with increasing data volume. Currently, the neural network architectures proposed for AMC tasks mainly fall into two categories: Long Short-Term Memory (LSTMs) [5], [6] and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [7]-[9]. Among them, the model proposed in [10] based on the residual network (ResNet) architecture [11] outperforms all other CNN architectures; furthermore, studies have balanced performance and computational speed through neural architecture search [12]-[14]. Although neural networks are currently the mainstream and most effective method for solving AMC problems [10], they are highly sensitive to adversarial perturbations, which could threaten the system's security under malicious attacks [9], [15]-[19].
Adversarial perturbations are deliberately designed but almost imperceptible disturbances added to transmission signals. Such perturbations can serve as an effective means of disrupting wireless communication systems due to their low energy requirements compared to conventional attacks. On the other hand, adversarial training [22], [23], random smoothing [23], [24], and generative adversarial networks [25] have been proven to effectively enhance the robustness of AMC models. However, these defense methods not only come with high computational costs but also significantly reduce the accuracy of models on clean data (unperturbed data).
Computer vision research has attempted to explore the reasons why networks are vulnerable to attacks, with label noise being considered one of the main factors [27]. Knowledge distillation [28]-[31] can mitigate this impact by training student models to fit the outputs of teacher models. Although knowledge distillation has been applied in AMC [32], computer vision research [33]-[36] indicates that for achieving adversarial robustness, the outputs of student models need to match those of teacher models on adversarial samples; and to significantly enhance robustness, both teacher and student models must undergo adversarial training [33], which brings about extremely high computational costs.
Based on the above observations, this study proposes a data-centric new method aimed at improving the accuracy of neural networks and their adversarial robustness. The research hypothesis: The information provided by the original labels is limited, unable to reflect the uncertainty difference between low and high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) signals. Therefore, this paper proposes estimating theoretical class probabilities obtained from offline ML functions using distilled offline ML functions to provide more rich information for the model. We use these calculated probabilities to train neural networks, transferring the excellent performance of ML functions, and then apply the trained network to online scenarios for inference, thereby avoiding relying solely on prior knowledge available only in offline scenarios.
Moreover, this method is particularly effective in training robust models: Since the performance of adversarial training highly depends on data quality, and distilled probabilities can provide higher-quality information, it can significantly improve training effects. Experiments show that compared to using raw labels or eliminating label noise, this knowledge distillation method can simultaneously improve model accuracy and adversarial robustness; combining this method with adversarial training (i.e., training models on adversarial samples to fit distilled labels), similar performance improvements can also be achieved. It is worth noting that although ML estimation is limited to offline scenarios, the trained models still exhibit significant advantages in online scenarios.
This study believes that enabling models to learn from uncertainty in complex scenarios is a highly promising research direction, with its value not limited to the AMC field. This further validates the intuitive notion that "the quality and selection of training data have a significant impact on the model's robustness against adversarial attacks."
III. Methodology Principle (I) Overall Framework The communication system corresponding to the AMC task consists of an emitter, a communication channel, and a receiver (gray module), as shown in Figure 1. The emitter includes a modulator and a communication filter: the modulator converts data into symbol sequences through a specific mapping, which are then upsampled by the communication filter (such as root-raised cosine filter) to avoid intersymbol interference (ISI); signals inevitably introduce noise and distortion when transmitted through the communication channel; the receiver contains another communication filter (for downsampling and converting signals back into symbols) and a demodulator (for converting symbols into data).
The core issue in AMC is automatically selecting the correct demodulator based on the received signal. There are two scenarios in practical applications:
1. 
Coherent scenario: known all system information, applicable only offline;
2. 
Non-coherent scenario: partially or fully unknown channels and transmitter characteristics, often used for online scenarios and real-world model performance testing.
When training neural networks for AMC or other tasks, it is common to optimize the model to be as confident and accurate on the training set as possible. The traditional AMC training set consists of the received signal and its corresponding modulation method label (a one-hot encoding vector). However, this type of label has limitations: for signals with strong noise and severe distortion, the actual modulation method represented by the received signal may differ from the one predicted by the model (this is a common occurrence in AMC-related literature model testing [37]). Therefore, it is necessary to train the model with better information to allow it to represent uncertainty through "low confidence predictions" - and the analytical model "Maximum Likelihood (ML) function" in the AMC domain can provide the required probability vector for training.

IV. Conclusion This study emphasizes that incorporating additional information provided by theoretical models such as maximum likelihood (ML) during neural network training has significant advantages. This is a key step in building more robust networks, helping to enhance the trustworthiness of models in critical scenarios. Experiments show that even when the network operates under different prior conditions, the robustness can still be improved by distilling these mathematical models to provide probabilities.
Future research can explore other knowledge distillation loss functions and adopt more adaptive ML functions for Rayleigh or Rician channels to further optimize results in general scenarios containing multiple channel types.
